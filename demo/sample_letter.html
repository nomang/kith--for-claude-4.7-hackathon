Dear Carol,

This was Ellie's first week with Kith, and I want to start by saying: she arrived on Monday tentative and left Sunday saying it had been a good week. That arc — nervous to certain — happened in seven days, and she earned it.

She is still very much herself. The library runs through everything she says, not as nostalgia but as a philosophy she has worked out over decades. She has opinions about what a library is for ("a place where quiet people discover they're not alone"), and about what it means to do your work entirely as yourself ("that's the only ambition worth having"). She still composes. She wrote a small poem on Friday about a maple tree on the Stowe road — the one that turns before everything else — and she said it wasn't finished, and she was wrong.

George is her anchor. When he was at Dr. Hartwell's on Wednesday, she asked where he was three times in twenty minutes. She wasn't distressed — she was just a little unmoored. She settled as soon as the garden came into the conversation, and by the end of the session she was talking about the mint with the authority of someone who has been in a long negotiation with it (she has: she planted it twenty years ago and considers herself to have lost). George came home and they walked out to the garden together that afternoon. The crisis, such as it was, resolved entirely.

“I said I wasn't sure it sounded grand enough for a Mary Oliver poem. And she said: I think it sounds exactly right.” — Ellie, on Lily's question: Sunday, April 20

Sunday was the best day. Lily called before breakfast to read “The Summer Day” for a school assignment and asked Ellie what her answer was — her plan for her one wild and precious life. Ellie answered: helping people find what they needed, the tea George makes every morning, the herb garden that returns. She told me she wasn't sure it was grand enough. I want you to know, Carol, it was exactly grand enough. It was the grandest thing anyone said to me all week.

There are a few things from this week worth your attention — not urgencies, but things I want you to know.

Five moments worth keeping
  • Monday — The lilac bush out front bloomed for the first time this spring. She almost called you to tell you. She should have. “A lilac blooming is worth a phone call.”
  • Tuesday — Explaining the lemon drizzle recipe, she arrived at the principle: "You have to trust the drizzle." She laughed when she said it. She said it should be a life principle. She wrote it on the recipe card years ago. It already is.
  • Thursday — Telling the story of Marcus and his dragon books, and then remembering Audrey Hepburn in Roman Holiday at age seven: "That's what a woman can be. Not loud. Not small. Just entirely herself." She has lived up to it.
  • Friday — She composed a fragment of her own poetry about the maple on the Stowe road. "Every October you lean into it, / the outrageous red of being / before the quiet comes." She said it wasn't finished. It is finished.
  • Saturday — Your visit. The recipe box. Ruth's handwritten cards. George at the table with his reading glasses on. "It was like the old days." That sentence came from a place of fullness, not longing.
<\!-- Section: For your attention -->
A few things for you

The recipe cards. You said you'd have them printed and framed. Please do. She mentioned it once and didn't press. She is not a presser. But it matters to her that those cards — especially the ones in Ruth's faded pencil — are cared for. If you have them printed, tell her. She'll want to know they're safe.

George's appointments. If George has a scheduled appointment, a quick note to Kith beforehand with the specifics — who, what time, when he'll be back — helps significantly. Not vague reassurance: the actual anchor. "He went to see Dr. Hartwell. He'll bring the mail when he gets home." That's the difference between a settled morning and Wednesday.

Lily and Mary Oliver. Lily was reading Mary Oliver independently this summer — I can see it in the Shoebox photos from Portland. She doesn't know this yet, but she and her grandmother have been reading the same poet from different ends of their lives. You might tell Lily that when you see her. It's a beautiful coincidence to be in possession of.

The library alcove. You mentioned in the onboarding notes that the Burlington library named a reading corner after Ellie in 2009 and that you were waiting for a good day to tell her. Carol: this was a good week. You'll know when the right day comes. But if you've been waiting for a sign that she's ready for good news — this week was it.
